# Results

## R-M systems undergo geographic redistribution without transcriptional consequences

To determine how DNA methylation interacts with the transcriptional program of *S. coelicolor* M145 during development, we first characterized the methylome across three developmental stages: exponential growth (T1), transition phase (T2), and stationary phase (T3). Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing across nine samples (three biological replicates per timepoint) detected two types of base modifications distributed across the 8.67 Mb linear chromosome: N4-methylcytosine (4mC; 3,294 high-confidence sites) and N6-methyladenine (6mA; 2,734 sites) (Fig. 1a, 1b). Both modification types showed dramatic temporal dynamics, with 4mC sites declining from 1,289 at T1 to 407 at T2 and 21 at T3 (Fig. 1b). De novo motif discovery (MEME) on the high-confidence sites revealed two dominant recognition sequences: GCCGGC for 4mC and AAGCCCG for 6mA (Fig. 1d), which together account for the vast majority of detected modifications. Neither modification type showed preferential targeting of specific genomic features — promoter, coding, and intergenic regions were methylated in proportion to their genomic coverage (Fig. 1c) — indicating no initial evidence of promoter-specific regulation. The GCCGGC motif is a palindromic hexamer, and 91.4% of detected 4mC sites fell within this motif context, consistent with a Type II restriction-modification (R-M) system (Table S2). Notably, initial classification suggested possible 5-methylcytosine (5mC), but detailed analysis confirmed that the modification is 4mC (5mC signal was 0.01%), a modification type characteristic of R-M defense rather than epigenetic regulation (Fig. S1). Based on these observations, all subsequent analyses focus on the two identified R-M systems (GCCGGC 4mC and AAGCCCG 6mA) and their potential relationship to gene regulation.

The GCCGGC 4mC system exhibited dramatic geographic redistribution across the linear chromosome (Fig. 2a). At T1, 1,289 high-confidence 4mC sites were detected, with 83% located in the chromosomal core region (1.5–7.17 Mb). By T2, only 407 sites remained, with the geographic distribution inverted: 82% were now located in the arm regions. At T3, a mere 21 sites persisted (62% in arms). This core-to-arm shift was highly significant (chi-squared = 597, *p* ~ 10^-130). Critically, the T1 and T2 GCCGGC site sets were entirely non-overlapping (Jaccard index = 0.000), demonstrating active positional remodeling rather than passive dilution through DNA replication (Table S1). If the decrease in site number from T1 to T2 were due to dilution by replication, the T2 site set would be a subset of the T1 set; the complete non-overlap rules out this explanation. This positional turnover is consistent with the function of an R-M defense system whose target landscape shifts during the developmental cycle, potentially reflecting changes in the phage vulnerability profile of different chromosomal regions.

The AAGCCCG 6mA system showed a contrasting pattern: a 75% loss in site number (260 sites at T1 to 64 at T2) with minimal geographic shift (69% to 64% core, a 4.7 percentage-point change). Unlike the GCCGGC system, the AAGCCCG site loss was directly attributable to reduced methyltransferase expression. *SC_RS17645*, encoding an HsdM-type methyltransferase, was downregulated 4.6-fold (log2 fold change (LFC) = -2.19, T2 vs T1), and it was the only methyltransferase among 22 annotated MTase genes showing significant temporal change (Fig. 1, Table S3). The AAGCCCG motif is not a palindrome, which — together with the HsdM-type MTase — is consistent with a Type I R-M system.

Detailed analysis of the AAGCCCG recognition motif revealed that the second adenine (position 1 of the 7-mer) is the predominant methylation target, accounting for 63% (164/260) of AAGCCCG-matching sites at T1, with the first adenine (position 0) contributing 18% and the adjacent guanine (position 2) accounting for 19% (Fig. S14a). This specificity is consistent with the canonical HsdM-type methyltransferase mechanism, in which the enzyme methylates a single specific base within the recognition sequence. For GCCGGC, the palindromic nature of the motif was confirmed by near-equal methylation at positions 1 and 3 (53% and 47% respectively), corresponding to the cytosine residues at each end of the palindromic core (Fig. S14b). We further quantified the fraction of genomic recognition sites that are actually methylated — "motif occupancy" — finding striking differences between the two systems: at T1, AAGCCCG occupancy was 31.6% (447 of 1,414 genomic AAGCCCG sites), GCCGGC occupancy was 7.96% (1,307 of 16,427 sites), and GATC occupancy was 0.27% (121 of 45,569 sites) (Fig. S14c). The sub-complete occupancy of GCCGGC and AAGCCCG sites implies either physical inaccessibility of recognition sites (e.g., due to protein occupancy) or processivity limitations of the cognate methyltransferases. Finally, 72.2% of 6mA sites at T1 (1,396 of 1,934) did not match canonical AAGCCCG, GATC, or their reverse complements within a ±15 bp window — a fraction that increased to 80.3% at T2 and 82.7% at T3 (Fig. S14d). These "unassigned" 6mA sites suggest the existence of at least one additional, uncharacterized MTase in *S. coelicolor* M145 whose recognition sequence remains unknown.

Despite these dramatic temporal changes, neither methylation system showed evidence of transcriptional regulation when appropriate controls were applied. For GCCGGC 4mC, we compared the expression changes of genes that lost proximal methylation between T1 and T2 ("Lost," n = 3,226) versus genes that were never methylated ("Never," n = 4,180). The unstratified analysis yielded an apparently significant result (*p* = 8.3 x 10^-8), with Lost genes showing a slight negative median LFC shift (-0.259). However, geographic stratification revealed this as a Simpson's paradox artifact: within the core region alone, the effect vanished entirely (core-only *p* = 0.87, median LFC difference = +0.022) (Fig. S9a). The confound arises because T1 GCCGGC sites are 83% core-located, and core genes have systematically lower LFC at T2 irrespective of methylation status — a consequence of the core/arm functional differentiation of the linear chromosome. For AAGCCCG 6mA, which has minimal geographic bias, the test provided a clean null result: Lost (n = 836) versus Never (n = 6,397) genes showed no difference in expression change (*r* = 0.003, *p* = 0.91) (Fig. S9b). The AAGCCCG result is particularly informative because the absence of geographic confound means no stratification correction is needed, providing the most direct test of whether methylation loss causes de-repression. We conclude that both methylation systems function as R-M defense components whose temporal dynamics reflect chromosomal defense remodeling, not transcriptional regulation.

To characterize the spatial distribution of methylation dynamics across the chromosome, we computed methylation density in 50 kb windows genome-wide at each timepoint (Fig. S15). The 4mC density showed a characteristic non-monotonic temporal trajectory: an increase from T1 (mean 0.229 sites/kb) to T2 (0.283 sites/kb) followed by a sharp decline to T3 (0.191 sites/kb). This T2 peak coincides with the geographic flattening of core/arm inequality, as the core-to-arm density ratio dropped from 2.21 at T1 to 1.12 at T2 before partially recovering at T3 (1.32), suggesting that R-M defense activity expands genome-wide at the developmental transition before retreating. In contrast, 6mA density increased monotonically across all timepoints (0.223 → 0.245 → 0.264 sites/kb) with consistently uniform geographic distribution (core/arm ratio ≈ 1.0 at all timepoints), consistent with a temporally accumulating modification that is deposited without geographic preference (Fig. S15).

## Methylation is selectively depleted from regulatory gene promoters

Having established that methylation does not directly regulate transcription genome-wide, we asked whether the distribution of methylation sites relative to gene regulatory regions reveals a structural relationship between the epigenome and the regulatory architecture. This question is distinct from the temporal analysis above: rather than asking "does methylation change cause expression change?", we ask "is methylation spatially excluded from regulatory regions?" We focused on regulatory genes because, if methylation has any structural relationship to the transcriptional program, it should be most apparent at the genes that encode transcriptional regulators themselves.

We annotated 1,055 regulatory genes across the M145 genome — including transcription factors (TFs), two-component system (TCS) components, and sigma factors (see Methods) — and computed methylation density profiles centered on the transcription start sites (TSSs) of these genes compared to all 7,646 expressed genes. TSS positions were assigned from the NCBI RefSeq annotation, supplemented with experimentally determined TSSs from dRNA-seq data where available (262 genes). Regulatory gene TSSs showed a pronounced zone of methylation depletion spanning approximately 2,200 bp, from -1,300 to +700 bp relative to the TSS (Fig. 2b). The deepest depletion occurred at +300 bp downstream of the TSS, where methylation density dropped to 54.1% of flanking region levels (Fig. 3a). Non-regulatory genes showed no comparable depletion, and the effect was specific to regulatory genes among all functional categories tested: ribosomal protein genes, DNA repair genes, and energy metabolism genes showed no significant depletion (*p* > 0.05 for all). This protection zone was observed for both GCCGGC and AAGCCCG methylation types, indicating that it is not specific to a single R-M system but reflects a general property of regulatory gene promoter architecture.

Crucially, this regulatory avoidance pattern survived geographic stratification. The Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) test, which controls for core/arm localization, confirmed that the depletion of methylation sites from regulatory gene regions was genuine and not a geographic artifact (CMH-adjusted odds ratio = 0.60 for GCCGGC at regulatory genes, *p* = 2.9 x 10^-5; CMH-adjusted odds ratio = 0.41 for AAGCCCG 6mA, *p* = 0.003) (Fig. S3). This result distinguishes the regulatory avoidance pattern from the Simpson's paradox artifacts described above: while the temporal methylation-expression correlations are geographic confounds, the spatial exclusion of methylation from regulatory promoters is a genuine structural feature.

## A two-tier mechanism generates the protection zone

We next investigated the mechanism underlying the protection zone by testing two candidate explanations: evolutionary counter-selection of R-M recognition motifs from regulatory DNA, and physical protection through protein occupancy at promoters.

Sequence analysis revealed that regulatory gene bodies are depleted for R-M recognition motif sequences at the DNA level, independent of whether those motifs are methylated (fold = 0.74–0.76 relative to non-regulatory genes, *p* < 3 x 10^-7) (Fig. S4). This depletion is further enhanced at the TSSs of exposed regulators (see below). A combined logistic regression model incorporating all sequence features (AAGCCCG count, TGGCCGGC/GCCGGC count, and combined features within ±300 bp of the TSS) achieved a 5-fold cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.712 ± 0.049 (Fig. 3c). The strongest single-sequence discriminator was the AAGCCCG motif, which was 5.0-fold enriched within ±300 bp of exposed TF TSSs compared to shielded TF TSSs (*p* = 1.1 x 10^-8); the TGGCCGGC palindrome was 2.66-fold enriched (*p* = 4.4 x 10^-4). These sequence contributions account for approximately 33% of the total discriminative power between exposed and shielded regulatory genes, consistent with an evolutionary counter-selection model in which shielded regulators have evolved reduced motif density near their promoters while exposed regulators retain ancestral motif frequencies.

The remaining ~67% of the protection cannot be explained by DNA sequence and is instead attributed to collective protein occupancy at regulatory gene promoters. Several converging lines of evidence support this interpretation. First, the nearest methylation site distance from the TSS — a measure of physical methylation exclusion — achieved an AUC of 0.917 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.895–0.935), far exceeding the sequence model (AUC = 0.712) (Fig. 3b, 3c). The gap between these two AUCs (0.917 - 0.712 = 0.205) represents the contribution of non-sequence factors, which we attribute to protein occupancy. Second, this protection was independent of expression level: the mean expression level (baseMean) achieved an AUC of only 0.547 (near random) for predicting exposed versus shielded status, and no trend was observed across expression quintiles (Jonckheere-Terpstra (JT) test, *p* = 0.730) (Fig. 3e). This rules out an RNA polymerase occupancy model in which highly expressed genes are protected simply because their promoters are frequently occupied by transcription machinery. Third, and most surprisingly, individual TF binding sites (BSs) were not protective. Analysis of methylation density around computationally predicted TF BSs using FIMO (Find Individual Motif Occurrences) showed flat profiles with no depletion; GCCGGC sites were in fact slightly enriched at TF BSs (fold = 1.157, *p* = 0.006), a counter-intuitive result suggesting that known TF binding, if anything, attracts rather than repels methylation (Fig. 3d). These results rule out a model in which specific TF binding physically excludes methyltransferases from individual regulatory sites. Instead, the data point to a constitutive protein architecture at promoter regions — comprising nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) such as HupA, IHF, Lsr2, and Gbn, together with the basal transcription apparatus — that collectively and redundantly shields regulatory DNA from methyltransferase access. This shielding operates through physical steric occlusion by the resident protein complement rather than through active transcription: even infrequently transcribed regulatory genes maintain protection, as demonstrated by the near-random AUC of expression level for predicting exposed/shielded status (0.547). Although most of these NAPs bind DNA in a largely sequence-nonspecific manner, at least one — Gbn — recognizes a specific motif (GATCWT) with >2,800 chromosomal binding regions covering ~55% of all GATCWT sites genome-wide [CITE: Du et al., 2022]. The protection zone thus reflects collective, multi-layered shielding in which the loss of any single protein's occupancy is compensated by the continued binding of others, rather than the action of a single dominant factor.

Analysis of TSS-centered methylation profiles across all three developmental timepoints revealed that the protection zone itself undergoes temporal remodeling (Fig. S16). For 4mC, the protection ratio (defined as mean density within the -500 to 0 bp upstream window divided by mean density within the -2,000 to -500 bp window; values < 1 indicate depletion) weakened progressively from T1 (ratio = 0.823) to T3 (ratio = 0.867), reflecting the overall decline in 4mC density. However, this average trend concealed a striking gene-category-specific divergence: among regulatory genes, the 4mC protection ratio collapsed to 1.046 at T3 — a value exceeding 1.0, indicating that by late development the region immediately upstream of regulatory gene TSSs is, if anything, *enriched* for 4mC relative to the flanking regions (Fig. S16c, d). Non-regulatory genes showed no comparable collapse (ratio = 0.821 at T1 to 0.845 at T3), demonstrating that the protection zone breakdown is specific to the regulatory gene class. For 6mA, the regulatory gene protection was deepest at T2 (ratio = 0.732), with partial recovery at T3 (0.764), consistent with the known increase in total 6mA density at T2–T3. Together, these temporal dynamics suggest that the methylation protection zone at regulatory gene promoters is not static but undergoes a developmental-stage-specific reorganization, with the 4mC protection collapsing specifically in the regulatory gene class at the onset of stationary phase.

## A sharp 293 bp boundary defines the Shielded/Exposed dichotomy

The protection zone creates a remarkably sharp binary classification among regulatory genes. Of 1,055 regulatory genes analyzed, 993 (94.1%) maintain a methylation-free zone around their TSS ("shielded regulators"), with a median nearest methylation site distance of 762 bp. The remaining 62 (5.9%) lack this protection entirely ("exposed regulators"), with methylation sites directly at their TSS (median distance = 114 bp) and an 8.4-fold higher TSS methylation density (3.15 vs 0.37 sites per kb per gene) (Fig. 2c). It is important to note that the "nearest methylation distance" measured here is the genomic distance from the TSS to the closest experimentally detected DNA methylation modification site — a metric of local epigenomic accessibility — and should not be confused with the distance from the TSS to the translation initiation site (i.e., the 5' UTR length).

The separation between these two classes is statistically overwhelming (Mann-Whitney *U* test, *p* = 5.3 x 10^-28), with a 6.7-fold ratio in median nearest methylation distance. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified an optimal distance threshold of 293 bp (Youden's *J* statistic), achieving 100% sensitivity and 80.6% specificity (AUC = 0.917, 95% CI: 0.895–0.935) (Fig. 3b). This near-perfect single-feature classification demonstrates that the Shielded/Exposed dichotomy is a binary structural property of the regulatory genome, not a graded continuum.

The two classes differ not only in methylation proximity but also in their transcriptional behavior. None of the 62 exposed regulators showed constitutive expression across the three developmental stages (0%), compared to 38% of shielded regulators (odds ratio = infinity, *p* = 5.3 x 10^-13 by Fisher's exact test). The exposed regulators also showed 1.7-fold larger absolute expression changes (median |LFC| T3 vs T1: 1.53 for exposed vs 0.93 for shielded), consistent with their role as dynamic regulators at the developmental boundary. The exposed set showed weak but significant enrichment in the arm regions (56.5% core vs 63.8% core for shielded), though this difference did not reach significance (*p* = 0.277). TF family composition differed between the two classes: exposed regulators were enriched for HTH (other) and sensor kinase annotations, reflecting the diversity of uncharacterized regulators in this set (Table S5).

An important question is whether exposed regulators represent evolutionarily recent additions to the genome — genes that have not yet evolved protection — or established regulatory genes that happen to reside in methylation-accessible genomic contexts. All available evolutionary proxy measures indicate the latter. Exposed and shielded regulators showed no significant difference in SCO locus tag assignment rate (96.8% vs 97.8%), named product rate (100% vs 100%), GC content at third codon positions (0.919 vs 0.932, *p* = 0.060), effective number of codons (31.6 vs 31.5, *p* = 0.725), or rare codon frequency (0.032 vs 0.027, *p* = 0.122). A composite conservation score yielded an ROC AUC of 0.459 — below random chance — confirming that evolutionary divergence metrics have no power to predict exposed status (Fig. S7). The Shielded/Exposed distinction is thus determined by the local epigenomic context — the density and proximity of R-M recognition sites relative to the TSS — not by the gene's evolutionary history or functional importance.

## 62 exposed regulators form a distributed developmental switch

If the 62 exposed regulators are the sole conduit through which methylation interacts with the transcriptional program, understanding their internal organization and biological function becomes essential. Co-expression analysis across all timepoints and replicates revealed that these 62 genes are not a monolithic group but contain strong internal structure organized into two antagonistic blocs — a repression bloc of collectively downregulated genes and an activation bloc of collectively upregulated genes (Fig. 4a, 4b).

Hierarchical clustering of the 62 x 62 Spearman co-expression matrix identified four modules covering 61 of 62 genes (one sigma factor gene unassigned). Within-type co-expression (gene pairs in the same bloc) was strongly positive (median *rho* = 0.500, *p* = 6.6 x 10^-25), while between-type co-expression (gene pairs across blocs) was negative (median *rho* = -0.283), producing the characteristic mirror-image temporal trajectories of two antagonistic gene sets. Module-internal coherence was even stronger (median *rho* = 0.717, *p* = 1.0 x 10^-19), confirming that the four modules represent tightly coordinated sub-networks. Importantly, these 62 genes are not physically clustered on the chromosome (genomic clustering *z* = +0.30, *p* = 0.37), include zero operonic pairs (*p* = 1.0), and are distributed across both core and arm regions. Their co-regulation arises through trans-acting mechanisms rather than genomic proximity, distinguishing this regulatory layer from the pathway-specific gene clusters common in *Streptomyces*.

The **activation bloc** (~35 genes, modules 1–3) is enriched for regulators of developmental programs. This bloc includes nine TCS components (25% of bloc members), five sigma factors, one WhiB-family regulator, and the aerial mycelium regulator *ramR*. The mean LFC (T3 vs T1) for the activation bloc was +2.47, indicating strong collective upregulation during development. The most dramatically induced genes include *ramR* (SCO6685, peak LFC = +7.9 at T3 vs T1), encoding a key activator of aerial mycelium formation that controls the production of the surfactant peptide SapB; *nsdB* (SCO7252, peak LFC = +9.3 at T2 vs T1), a DNA-binding protein whose strong early induction may serve as a developmental checkpoint activator; and *SCO1160* (peak LFC = +6.8 at T2 vs T1), encoding a sensor kinase whose cognate response regulator is shielded (Fig. 3b; see inset). Additional notable members include multiple sigma-70 family sigma factors that could direct RNA polymerase to developmental promoters, and WhiB-like regulators involved in sporulation. The activation bloc collectively drives the onset of morphogenesis, signal transduction cascades, and stress response programs (Fig. 4b).

The **repression bloc** (~26 genes, module 4) is enriched for regulators of vegetative growth functions. The mean LFC (T3 vs T1) was -1.68, reflecting coordinated downregulation. The TetR family of transcriptional regulators is the only statistically significantly enriched family in this bloc (odds ratio = 0.16, *p* = 0.028 by Fisher's exact test), with seven TetR members present. This is consistent with the established role of TetR-family proteins in regulating efflux pumps, metabolite transport, and small-molecule resistance — functions characteristic of active vegetative growth that must be shut down during the transition to development. The repression bloc also includes GntR, IclR, and LacI family regulators involved in metabolic control, as well as SSB and HU proteins participating in DNA maintenance and repair (Fig. 4a). The coordinated downregulation of this bloc corresponds to the shutdown of primary metabolism, DNA repair capacity, and active efflux at the onset of the developmental program.

Given the prominence of TCS components among the 62 exposed regulators (9 of 62, 14.5%), we examined whether cognate TCS pairs — in which a sensor kinase (SK) is paired with a response regulator (RR) — show coordinated or asymmetric exposed/shielded status. Among the 62 exposed regulators, seven are members of identified cognate TCS pairs. In every case (7/7), exactly one member of the pair is exposed while its cognate partner is shielded (Fig. 3e). We emphasize that this is a descriptive observation: a formal permutation test shuffling exposed/shielded labels across all 84 adjacent SK-RR pairs in M145 yielded *p* = 0.83, reflecting the fact that with a background exposure rate of only ~7%, observing zero co-exposed pairs is expected by chance (expected co-exposed pairs under the null = 0.37). The small sample size (7 identified pairs) precludes a definitive statistical test. Among the exposed partners, the split is 4 SK to 3 RR, showing no bias toward either component type (*p* = 1.0). Despite the lack of statistical power, the consistent pattern is biologically interpretable: in a TCS, the sensor kinase detects environmental signals while the response regulator executes the transcriptional output. The asymmetric exposure of one component per pair is consistent with a model in which methylation accessibility differentially gates the input and output arms of these signaling pathways — a hypothesis warranting future experimental validation with larger TCS datasets.

To characterize the functional landscape of genes flanked by each methylation type, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the three gene proximity classes: GCCGGC-proximal (n = 3,197), AAGCCCG-proximal (n = 459), and dual-targeted (n = 448) (Fisher's exact test + Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction). GCCGGC-proximal genes were significantly enriched for the KEGG Quorum Sensing pathway (65/100 pathway genes, enrichment fold = 1.55, FDR = 2.5 × 10^-4) and showed a trend toward Two-Component System enrichment (64/116 genes, fold = 1.32, FDR = 0.139). The Quorum Sensing enrichment implies that a disproportionate fraction of the genes encoding signaling and regulatory communication functions reside in close proximity to GCCGGC 4mC sites — consistent with the broader role of the R-M system in shaping the methylation landscape around signal transduction machinery. Dual-targeted genes showed a striking enrichment for siderophore biosynthesis (5/7 pathway genes, fold = 12.2, FDR = 8.7 × 10^-4), as well as folate biosynthesis (fold = 5.1, FDR = 0.025), oxidative phosphorylation (fold = 2.8, FDR = 0.032), and peptidoglycan biosynthesis (fold = 3.7, FDR = 0.036) — all functions related to primary metabolic capacity and cellular envelope integrity during vegetative growth. In contrast, AAGCCCG-proximal genes showed no significant KEGG enrichment (0 pathways at FDR < 0.2), consistent with the uniform geographic distribution of 6mA sites and the absence of functional specificity in the AAGCCCG targeting landscape.

## Both blocs activate simultaneously at the vegetative-to-developmental transition

The temporal dynamics of the 62 exposed regulators reveal a coordinated, simultaneous switch at the T1-to-T2 developmental boundary (Fig. 3c). We quantified the timing of each gene's response using a phase ratio metric, defined as: phase ratio = |LFC T1→T2| / (|LFC T1→T2| + |LFC T2→T3|). A phase ratio > 0.6 indicates that more than 60% of the total expression change is concentrated in the first transition, qualifying the gene as an "early responder." Figure 3c plots the two transition magnitudes directly (x-axis: |LFC T1→T2|; y-axis: |LFC T2→T3|), with the phase ratio = 0.6 threshold shown as the dashed line (y = 0.67x; genes below the line are early-dominant). The majority of exposed regulators (63%, 39/62 genes) are early responders by this criterion, with the bulk of their transcriptional shift concentrated at the exponential-to-transition boundary. A smaller fraction showed gradual (8 genes) or late (12 genes) responses, and only 2 genes were non-responders.

The activation bloc transitions from low expression at T1 (mean *z*-score = -1.03) through intermediate levels at T2 (mean *z*-score = +0.23) to high expression at T3 (mean *z*-score = +0.79), with a mean LFC of +1.91 at the T1-to-T2 transition and +0.53 at T2-to-T3. The repression bloc shows the mirror-image trajectory: high at T1 (mean *z*-score = +1.28), declining through T2 (mean *z*-score = -0.48) to low at T3 (mean *z*-score = -0.81), with a mean LFC of -1.47 at T1-to-T2 and -0.21 at T2-to-T3. Critically, both blocs engage at the same time — a Mann-Whitney *U* test comparing phase ratios between blocs yielded *p* = 0.459, indicating no temporal separation between activation and repression (Fig. 4c). This simultaneity is consistent with a single "gate-opening" event at the developmental boundary rather than a sequential cascade in which one bloc triggers the other. Module-internal temporal coherence was high (Spearman *rho* = 0.717, *p* = 1.0 x 10^-19), confirming that genes within each bloc respond as coordinated units. However, cross-module temporal prediction was essentially zero, reinforcing the conclusion that the modules respond in parallel to a shared developmental signal.

Notably, the timing of methylation changes was decoupled from the timing of expression changes. The correlation between the magnitude of methylation change and expression change was not significant at either developmental transition (T1-T2: *rho* = 0.136, *p* = 0.299; T2-T3: *rho* = 0.012, *p* = 0.927). This temporal independence indicates that methylation provides the structural context — defining which genes are exposed to the regulatory environment — but does not provide the temporal signal that triggers the switch. The developmental "gate" opens once at the vegetative-to-developmental boundary, and the exposed regulators respond to developmental signals (likely metabolic, nutritional, or cell density-dependent cues) that are themselves independent of methylation dynamics.

## Negative results define the scope of the model

The Gatekeeper model is shaped as much by rejected hypotheses as by supported ones. We present four categories of negative results that are essential for defining the model's scope and ruling out alternative explanations (Fig. S9). Collectively, these results establish that while this study characterizes the temporal redistribution of DNA methylation and its structural relationship to the regulatory genome, a direct causal role for methylation in controlling gene expression could not be demonstrated.

**Simpson's paradox eliminates apparent genome-wide methylation-expression correlations.** We identified three independent instances of Simpson's paradox, all driven by the linear chromosome's core/arm functional differentiation (Fig. S9a, Fig. S2). The apparent suppressive effect of GCCGGC proximity on gene expression (*p* = 4.1 x 10^-10, unstratified) vanished when analysis was restricted to core-region genes (*p* = 0.87). The apparent de-repression of genes losing GCCGGC methylation (*p* = 8.3 x 10^-8, unstratified) was similarly eliminated by geographic stratification (core-only *p* = 0.87). A third instance involved the apparent enrichment of methylation sites in biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) (fold = 1.66, *p* = 4.4 x 10^-8, unstratified), which reduced to fold = 1.07 (not significant) in the core-only analysis — a consequence of the near-complete co-localization of both methylation sites and BGCs in the chromosomal core at T1. In each case, the confound has the same structure: a non-random spatial distribution of methylation sites along the chromosome combined with a non-random spatial distribution of gene functional properties produces a spurious correlation. The AAGCCCG system, which has minimal geographic bias (4.7 percentage-point shift), served as an internal control: its de-repression test yielded *p* = 0.91 with no geographic confound (Fig. S9b). These results establish that any methylation-expression analysis in organisms with linear chromosomes and core/arm functional differentiation must include geographic stratification (e.g., Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test) before causal claims can be made.

**DNA sequence enrichment does not equal methylation enrichment.** The AAGCCCG recognition motif is 5.0-fold enriched in the DNA sequence within ±300 bp of exposed TF TSSs compared to shielded TF TSSs, with 25.8% (~16/62) of exposed TFs carrying the motif near their TSS (Fig. S9c). This initially suggested that AAGCCCG methylation might directly mediate exposed TF regulation. However, only 3.2% (2/62) of exposed TFs have a methylated AAGCCCG site within ±500 bp of their TSS, and these two genes show expression changes in opposite directions. The eight-fold discrepancy between sequence presence (25.8%) and methylation presence (3.2%) arises because the AAGCCCG methyltransferase methylates only approximately 19.5% of its recognition sites genome-wide — a selectivity that is unexplained but consistent with the behavior of many Type I R-M systems. The sequence enrichment thus reflects an evolutionary or structural property of exposed regulatory DNA — reduced counter-selection against the motif, as expected for genes that lack the protection zone — rather than active methylation-mediated regulation. This distinction between sequence enrichment and functional methylation is critical and cautions against inferring regulatory mechanisms from motif frequency alone.

**Exposed regulators act in trans, not in cis.** In *Streptomyces*, pathway-specific regulators typically reside within or adjacent to the gene clusters they regulate (e.g., ActII-ORF4 within the actinorhodin BGC). If exposed TFs function similarly as cis-regulators of adjacent genes, their genomic neighbors should show correlated expression changes. A permutation test (10,000 permutations) showed no evidence for this: the observed mean absolute LFC of neighbors (±10 genes from each exposed TF) was well within the null distribution (*p* = 0.857) (Fig. S9d). Furthermore, there was no distance decay in the correlation between an exposed TF's expression change and that of its neighbors (Spearman *rho* = +0.026, not significant). The transcriptional concordance rate of exposed TF neighbors (59.9%) was not significantly higher than that of shielded TF neighbors (55.6%, *p* = 0.191). These results establish that the 62 exposed regulators exert their effects through trans-acting mechanisms on genomically dispersed targets, distinguishing them from the cis-regulatory paradigm that dominates secondary metabolism regulation in *Streptomyces*.

**The proximate regulatory mechanism remains unidentified.** Despite the strong evidence for coordinated regulation and functional coherence, the mechanism through which the 62 exposed TFs exert their regulatory effects could not be identified computationally. None of the 62 exposed TFs have curated binding motifs in available databases (0/62 in FIMO analysis using CollecTF and CIS-BP databases). While 38 of 62 are themselves targets of known TFs identified by FIMO, this rate is indistinguishable from that of shielded regulators (odds ratio = 0.881, *p* = 0.683), ruling out preferential targeting by master regulators as the coordination mechanism. The AAGCCCG methylation mechanism was eliminated as described above (2/62 methylated). The neighborhood effect was eliminated by permutation analysis. The upstream TF regulation model was also tested: target versus non-target absolute LFC showed no difference (*p* = 0.891), indicating that known TF cascades do not drive the observed coordination. We conclude that the 62 exposed regulators constitute a parallel regulatory layer that operates independently of all computationally characterized transcriptional networks in *S. coelicolor*. Importantly, the temporal decoupling between methylation dynamics and expression changes (T1-T2: *rho* = 0.136, *p* = 0.299; T2-T3: *rho* = 0.012, *p* = 0.927) precludes a simple causal model in which methylation directly triggers the observed transcriptional switch. Methylation may instead define the structural accessibility of regulatory DNA — setting the stage for which regulators can respond — while the actual transcriptional signal originates from developmental cues that are themselves independent of methylation. Identification of these proximate signals — whether metabolic, nutritional, or cell-density-dependent — and verification of the causal link between methylation accessibility and gene regulation will require experimental approaches including chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq), and targeted genetic knockouts of key exposed regulators such as *ramR* and *nsdB*.
