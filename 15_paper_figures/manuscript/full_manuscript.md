# Abstract

Bacterial DNA methylation is widely assumed to regulate transcription, yet genome-wide evidence for this function beyond a few model systems remains limited. Here we integrate Oxford Nanopore methylome and RNA-seq data across three developmental stages of *Streptomyces coelicolor* A3(2) strain M145 to systematically test whether two active methylation systems — GCCGGC (N4-methylcytosine) and AAGCCCG (N6-methyladenine) — function as transcriptional regulators.

We show that apparent genome-wide methylation-expression correlations are Simpson's paradox artifacts driven by the linear chromosome's core/arm functional differentiation: all three significant associations identified in unstratified analyses vanish upon geographic stratification (e.g., GCCGGC de-repression: *p* = 8.3 x 10^-8 unstratified, *p* = 0.87 core-only). Instead, we discover a four-layer "Gatekeeper" architecture. The restriction-modification systems undergo complete geographic redistribution (T1-T2 Jaccard = 0.000) without transcriptional consequences. A ~2,200 bp methylation-free protection zone at regulatory gene promoters, created by evolutionary counter-selection (~33%) and collective protein occupancy (~67%), shields 94.1% of regulatory genes (993/1,055). The remaining 62 exposed regulators (5.9%), defined by a 293 bp distance boundary (AUC = 0.917), constitute a distributed vegetative-to-developmental switch. These 62 genes are organized into two antagonistic blocs — an activation bloc (TCS, sigma factors, RamR) and a repression bloc (TetR-enriched, *p* = 0.028) — that engage simultaneously at the exponential-to-transition boundary.

DNA methylation does not control transcription directly in *S. coelicolor*. Rather, a structural dichotomy at regulatory gene promoters channels methylation effects through a specific minority of regulators encoding a coordinated developmental transition, revealing a previously unrecognized organizational principle of bacterial regulatory genomes.

# Introduction

DNA methylation is one of the most ancient and widespread epigenetic modifications in bacteria. Restriction-modification (R-M) systems, comprising a methyltransferase (MTase) and a cognate restriction endonuclease, defend bacteria against foreign DNA by methylating self-DNA at specific recognition sequences while cleaving unmethylated invading DNA [CITE: Tock and Dryden, 2005]. Beyond this well-established defense function, bacterial DNA methylation has been implicated in transcriptional regulation at specific loci. The Dam methylase (GATC, N6-methyladenine [6mA]) of *Escherichia coli* regulates DNA replication initiation, mismatch repair, and phase variation of surface antigens through differential methylation of promoter-proximal GATC sites [CITE: Casadesus and Low, 2006]. The CcrM methylase (GANTC, 6mA) of *Caulobacter crescentus* coordinates cell cycle-dependent gene expression by coupling replication-dependent hemimethylation to transcriptional activation [CITE: Gonzalez et al., 2014]. These characterized regulatory roles involve orphan methyltransferases — those lacking a cognate restriction endonuclease — at specific genomic loci, raising an important question: do R-M-associated methylation systems, which are far more numerous than orphan MTases across bacterial genomes, also contribute to transcriptional regulation? Genome-wide surveys using single-molecule real-time sequencing and nanopore sequencing have revealed extensive methylation landscapes across diverse bacterial species [CITE: Blow et al., 2016; Beaulaurier et al., 2019], but the extent to which these patterns reflect direct regulatory relationships — as opposed to defense system dynamics, geographic confounds, or other non-regulatory functions — remains largely untested.

*Streptomyces coelicolor* A3(2) provides an ideal system for investigating the relationship between DNA methylation and transcriptional regulation. This model actinobacterium possesses a large (8.67 Mb) linear chromosome with a distinctive core/arm architecture: the central core region (approximately 1.5–7.17 Mb) harbors most essential and housekeeping genes, while the terminal arm regions contain dispensable genes including biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) for secondary metabolites [CITE: Bentley et al., 2002]. *S. coelicolor* undergoes a complex developmental cycle that transitions from vegetative hyphal growth through aerial mycelium formation to sporulation, accompanied by the temporally coordinated activation of secondary metabolite biosynthesis [CITE: Flardh and Buttner, 2009; Hopwood, 2007]. This developmental program is governed by a well-characterized hierarchy of transcriptional regulators, including the Bld cascade (bldA, bldH/adpA) that initiates aerial development, the Whi regulators (whiB, whiG, whiH) that control sporulation, multiple developmental sigma factor cascades, and pathway-specific activators of the SARP family for individual BGCs such as actinorhodin and undecylprodigiosin [CITE: Bush et al., 2015; Higo et al., 2012]. Despite decades of research on *Streptomyces* transcriptional regulation and the availability of genome-wide functional genomics tools, no previous study has systematically examined how DNA methylation interacts with this regulatory architecture during the developmental cycle.

We characterized the methylome of *S. coelicolor* A3(2) strain M145 across three developmental stages using Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing, identifying two major methylation systems: GCCGGC (N4-methylcytosine [4mC]) with 1,289 high-confidence sites at exponential phase (T1) and AAGCCCG (N6-methyladenine [6mA]) with 260 sites. Both systems exhibit dramatic temporal dynamics: GCCGGC 4mC undergoes a 60-fold reduction in site number (1,289 to 21 sites) accompanied by a complete geographic inversion from core to arm regions, while AAGCCCG 6mA shows a 75% loss correlated with methyltransferase downregulation. Parallel RNA-seq across the same three developmental timepoints (three biological replicates each, nine samples total) provides a matched transcriptomic dataset. The initial expectation — that these dramatic temporal changes in methylation would correspond to coordinated changes in gene expression — motivated our systematic investigation.

A critical challenge in interpreting methylation-expression relationships in *S. coelicolor* arises from the linear chromosome's core/arm architecture. Because both methylation site distribution and gene expression profiles vary systematically along the chromosome — with T1 GCCGGC methylation sites concentrated in the core (83%) and core genes showing distinct developmental expression dynamics compared to arm genes — any correlation between methylation proximity and expression change may reflect geographic co-localization rather than a causal relationship. This type of confound is an instance of Simpson's paradox, in which an apparent association observed in aggregate data reverses or disappears when the data are stratified by a confounding variable [CITE: Simpson, 1951; Blyth, 1972]. The potential for Simpson's paradox in linear chromosome epigenomics has not been previously recognized, yet it has profound implications: without geographic stratification, standard analytical approaches would incorrectly conclude that GCCGGC methylation suppresses gene expression, that methylation loss causes de-repression, and that methylation is enriched in biosynthetic gene clusters.

Through a systematic, hypothesis-driven investigation spanning 36 hypotheses tested across 18 iterative analysis cycles, we discovered that the prevailing assumption — that DNA methylation directly regulates transcription genome-wide — is incorrect for *S. coelicolor* M145. The apparent genome-wide methylation-expression correlations are Simpson's paradox artifacts that vanish upon geographic stratification. Instead, the relationship between methylation and transcription is mediated by a four-layer "Gatekeeper" architecture: (1) R-M defense systems undergo geographic redistribution without transcriptional consequences; (2) 94.1% of regulatory gene promoters (993/1,055) maintain a ~2,200 bp methylation-free protection zone created by the combined action of evolutionary motif counter-selection (~33%) and collective promoter protein occupancy (~67%); (3) the remaining 5.9% of regulatory genes — 62 "exposed regulators" — lack this protection, creating a sharp binary dichotomy defined by a 293 bp threshold (AUC = 0.917); and (4) these 62 exposed regulators form a distributed developmental switch composed of two antagonistic blocs that simultaneously activate developmental programs and repress vegetative programs at the exponential-to-transition boundary.

Here we present the complete Gatekeeper model, including the quantitative characterization of the protection zone, the Shielded/Exposed dichotomy, the internal organization and temporal dynamics of the 62 exposed regulators, and the Simpson's paradox instances that shaped the model. The central discovery — that 12 rejected hypotheses about direct methylation-transcription control were required to reveal the true, structurally mediated pathway through 62 specific regulatory genes — underscores the value of systematic negative results in building mechanistic models of bacterial epigenomic function.

# Results

## R-M systems undergo geographic redistribution without transcriptional consequences

To determine how DNA methylation interacts with the transcriptional program of *S. coelicolor* M145 during development, we first characterized the methylome across three developmental stages: exponential growth (T1), transition phase (T2), and stationary phase (T3). Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing across nine samples (three biological replicates per timepoint) detected two types of base modifications distributed across the 8.67 Mb linear chromosome: N4-methylcytosine (4mC; 3,294 high-confidence sites) and N6-methyladenine (6mA; 2,734 sites) (Fig. 1a, 1b). Both modification types showed dramatic temporal dynamics, with 4mC sites declining from 1,289 at T1 to 407 at T2 and 21 at T3 (Fig. 1b). De novo motif discovery (MEME) on the high-confidence sites revealed two dominant recognition sequences: GCCGGC for 4mC and AAGCCCG for 6mA (Fig. 1d), which together account for the vast majority of detected modifications. Neither modification type showed preferential targeting of specific genomic features — promoter, coding, and intergenic regions were methylated in proportion to their genomic coverage (Fig. 1c) — indicating no initial evidence of promoter-specific regulation. The GCCGGC motif is a palindromic hexamer, and 91.4% of detected 4mC sites fell within this motif context, consistent with a Type II restriction-modification (R-M) system (Table S2). Notably, initial classification suggested possible 5-methylcytosine (5mC), but detailed analysis confirmed that the modification is 4mC (5mC signal was 0.01%), a modification type characteristic of R-M defense rather than epigenetic regulation (Fig. S1). Based on these observations, all subsequent analyses focus on the two identified R-M systems (GCCGGC 4mC and AAGCCCG 6mA) and their potential relationship to gene regulation.

The GCCGGC 4mC system exhibited dramatic geographic redistribution across the linear chromosome (Fig. 2a). At T1, 1,289 high-confidence 4mC sites were detected, with 83% located in the chromosomal core region (1.5–7.17 Mb). By T2, only 407 sites remained, with the geographic distribution inverted: 82% were now located in the arm regions. At T3, a mere 21 sites persisted (62% in arms). This core-to-arm shift was highly significant (chi-squared = 597, *p* ~ 10^-130). Critically, the T1 and T2 GCCGGC site sets were entirely non-overlapping (Jaccard index = 0.000), demonstrating active positional remodeling rather than passive dilution through DNA replication (Table S1). If the decrease in site number from T1 to T2 were due to dilution by replication, the T2 site set would be a subset of the T1 set; the complete non-overlap rules out this explanation. This positional turnover is consistent with the function of an R-M defense system whose target landscape shifts during the developmental cycle, potentially reflecting changes in the phage vulnerability profile of different chromosomal regions.

The AAGCCCG 6mA system showed a contrasting pattern: a 75% loss in site number (260 sites at T1 to 64 at T2) with minimal geographic shift (69% to 64% core, a 4.7 percentage-point change). Unlike the GCCGGC system, the AAGCCCG site loss was directly attributable to reduced methyltransferase expression. *SC_RS17645*, encoding an HsdM-type methyltransferase, was downregulated 4.6-fold (log2 fold change (LFC) = -2.19, T2 vs T1), and it was the only methyltransferase among 22 annotated MTase genes showing significant temporal change (Fig. 1, Table S3). The AAGCCCG motif is not a palindrome, which — together with the HsdM-type MTase — is consistent with a Type I R-M system.

Detailed analysis of the AAGCCCG recognition motif revealed that the second adenine (position 1 of the 7-mer) is the predominant methylation target, accounting for 63% (164/260) of AAGCCCG-matching sites at T1, with the first adenine (position 0) contributing 18% and the adjacent position accounting for 19% (Fig. S14a). For GCCGGC, the palindromic nature of the motif was confirmed by near-equal methylation at positions 1 and 3 (53% and 47% respectively), corresponding to the cytosine residues at each end of the palindromic core (Fig. S14b). Motif occupancy — the fraction of genomic recognition sites that are actually methylated — was 31.6% for AAGCCCG, 7.96% for GCCGGC, and 0.27% for GATC at T1 (Fig. S14c). The sub-complete GCCGGC and AAGCCCG occupancy implies either physical inaccessibility of recognition sites or processivity limitations of the cognate methyltransferases. Notably, 72.2% of 6mA sites at T1 did not match canonical AAGCCCG, GATC, or their reverse complements within a ±15 bp window — a fraction increasing to 82.7% at T3 — suggesting the existence of at least one additional uncharacterized MTase (Fig. S14d).

To characterize the spatial distribution of methylation dynamics across the chromosome, we computed methylation density in 50 kb windows genome-wide at each timepoint (Fig. S15). The 4mC density showed a non-monotonic trajectory: increase from T1 (0.229 sites/kb) to T2 (0.283 sites/kb) followed by sharp decline to T3 (0.191 sites/kb). The core-to-arm density ratio dropped from 2.21 at T1 to 1.12 at T2 before partially recovering at T3 (1.32), suggesting that R-M defense activity expands genome-wide at the developmental transition. In contrast, 6mA density increased monotonically (0.223 → 0.245 → 0.264 sites/kb) with consistently uniform geographic distribution (core/arm ratio ≈ 1.0 at all timepoints) (Fig. S15).

Despite these dramatic temporal changes, neither methylation system showed evidence of transcriptional regulation when appropriate controls were applied. For GCCGGC 4mC, we compared the expression changes of genes that lost proximal methylation between T1 and T2 ("Lost," n = 3,226) versus genes that were never methylated ("Never," n = 4,180). The unstratified analysis yielded an apparently significant result (*p* = 8.3 x 10^-8), with Lost genes showing a slight negative median LFC shift (-0.259). However, geographic stratification revealed this as a Simpson's paradox artifact: within the core region alone, the effect vanished entirely (core-only *p* = 0.87, median LFC difference = +0.022) (Fig. 5a). The confound arises because T1 GCCGGC sites are 83% core-located, and core genes have systematically lower LFC at T2 irrespective of methylation status — a consequence of the core/arm functional differentiation of the linear chromosome. For AAGCCCG 6mA, which has minimal geographic bias, the test provided a clean null result: Lost (n = 836) versus Never (n = 6,397) genes showed no difference in expression change (*r* = 0.003, *p* = 0.91) (Fig. 5b). The AAGCCCG result is particularly informative because the absence of geographic confound means no stratification correction is needed, providing the most direct test of whether methylation loss causes de-repression. We conclude that both methylation systems function as R-M defense components whose temporal dynamics reflect chromosomal defense remodeling, not transcriptional regulation.

## Methylation is selectively depleted from regulatory gene promoters

Having established that methylation does not directly regulate transcription genome-wide,, we asked whether the distribution of methylation sites relative to gene regulatory regions reveals a structural relationship between the epigenome and the regulatory architecture. This question is distinct from the temporal analysis above: rather than asking "does methylation change cause expression change?", we ask "is methylation spatially excluded from regulatory regions?" We focused on regulatory genes because, if methylation has any structural relationship to the transcriptional program, it should be most apparent at the genes that encode transcriptional regulators themselves.

We annotated 1,055 regulatory genes across the M145 genome — including transcription factors (TFs), two-component system (TCS) components, and sigma factors (see Methods) — and computed methylation density profiles centered on the transcription start sites (TSSs) of these genes compared to all 7,646 expressed genes. TSS positions were assigned from the NCBI RefSeq annotation, supplemented with experimentally determined TSSs from dRNA-seq data where available (262 genes). Regulatory gene TSSs showed a pronounced zone of methylation depletion spanning approximately 2,200 bp, from -1,300 to +700 bp relative to the TSS (Fig. 2b). The deepest depletion occurred at +300 bp downstream of the TSS, where methylation density dropped to 54.1% of flanking region levels (Fig. 3a). Non-regulatory genes showed no comparable depletion, and the effect was specific to regulatory genes among all functional categories tested: ribosomal protein genes, DNA repair genes, and energy metabolism genes showed no significant depletion (*p* > 0.05 for all). This protection zone was observed for both GCCGGC and AAGCCCG methylation types, indicating that it is not specific to a single R-M system but reflects a general property of regulatory gene promoter architecture.

Crucially, this regulatory avoidance pattern survived geographic stratification. The Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) test, which controls for core/arm localization, confirmed that the depletion of methylation sites from regulatory gene regions was genuine and not a geographic artifact (CMH-adjusted odds ratio = 0.60 for GCCGGC at regulatory genes, *p* = 2.9 x 10^-5; CMH-adjusted odds ratio = 0.41 for AAGCCCG 6mA, *p* = 0.003) (Fig. S3). This result distinguishes the regulatory avoidance pattern from the Simpson's paradox artifacts described above: while the temporal methylation-expression correlations are geographic confounds, the spatial exclusion of methylation from regulatory promoters is a genuine structural feature.

## A two-tier mechanism generates the protection zone

We next investigated the mechanism underlying the protection zone by testing two candidate explanations: evolutionary counter-selection of R-M recognition motifs from regulatory DNA, and physical protection through protein occupancy at promoters.

Sequence analysis revealed that regulatory gene bodies are depleted for R-M recognition motif sequences at the DNA level, independent of whether those motifs are methylated (fold = 0.74–0.76 relative to non-regulatory genes, *p* < 3 x 10^-7) (Fig. S4). This depletion is further enhanced at the TSSs of exposed regulators (see below). A combined logistic regression model incorporating all sequence features (AAGCCCG count, TGGCCGGC/GCCGGC count, and combined features within ±300 bp of the TSS) achieved a 5-fold cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.712 ± 0.049 (Fig. 3c). The strongest single-sequence discriminator was the AAGCCCG motif, which was 5.0-fold enriched within ±300 bp of exposed TF TSSs compared to shielded TF TSSs (*p* = 1.1 x 10^-8); the TGGCCGGC palindrome was 2.66-fold enriched (*p* = 4.4 x 10^-4). These sequence contributions account for approximately 33% of the total discriminative power between exposed and shielded regulatory genes, consistent with an evolutionary counter-selection model in which shielded regulators have evolved reduced motif density near their promoters while exposed regulators retain ancestral motif frequencies.

The remaining ~67% of the protection cannot be explained by DNA sequence and is instead attributed to collective protein occupancy at regulatory gene promoters. Several converging lines of evidence support this interpretation. First, the nearest methylation site distance from the TSS — a measure of physical methylation exclusion — achieved an AUC of 0.917 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.895–0.935), far exceeding the sequence model (AUC = 0.712) (Fig. 3b, 3c). The gap between these two AUCs (0.917 - 0.712 = 0.205) represents the contribution of non-sequence factors, which we attribute to protein occupancy. Second, this protection was independent of expression level: the mean expression level (baseMean) achieved an AUC of only 0.547 (near random) for predicting exposed versus shielded status, and no trend was observed across expression quintiles (Jonckheere-Terpstra (JT) test, *p* = 0.730) (Fig. 3e). This rules out an RNA polymerase occupancy model in which highly expressed genes are protected simply because their promoters are frequently occupied by transcription machinery. Third, and most surprisingly, individual TF binding sites (BSs) were not protective. Analysis of methylation density around computationally predicted TF BSs using FIMO (Find Individual Motif Occurrences) showed flat profiles with no depletion; GCCGGC sites were in fact slightly enriched at TF BSs (fold = 1.157, *p* = 0.006), a counter-intuitive result suggesting that known TF binding, if anything, attracts rather than repels methylation (Fig. 3d). These results rule out a model in which specific TF binding physically excludes methyltransferases from individual regulatory sites. Instead, the data point to a constitutive protein architecture at promoter regions — comprising nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) such as HupA, IHF, Lsr2, and Gbn, together with the basal transcription apparatus — that collectively and redundantly shields regulatory DNA from methyltransferase access. This shielding operates through physical steric occlusion by the resident protein complement rather than through active transcription: even infrequently transcribed regulatory genes maintain protection, as demonstrated by the near-random AUC of expression level for predicting exposed/shielded status (0.547). Although most of these NAPs bind DNA in a largely sequence-nonspecific manner, at least one — Gbn — recognizes a specific motif (GATCWT) with >2,800 chromosomal binding regions covering ~55% of all GATCWT sites genome-wide [CITE: Du et al., 2022]. The protection zone thus reflects collective, multi-layered shielding in which the loss of any single protein's occupancy is compensated by the continued binding of others, rather than the action of a single dominant factor.

Analysis of TSS-centered methylation profiles across all three developmental timepoints revealed that the protection zone itself undergoes temporal remodeling (Fig. S16). For 4mC, the protection ratio (upstream -500 to 0 bp density / far-upstream -2,000 to -500 bp density) weakened from T1 (0.823) to T3 (0.867), but concealed a striking gene-category-specific divergence: among regulatory genes, the 4mC protection ratio collapsed to 1.046 at T3 — exceeding 1.0, indicating that by late development the TSS-proximal region of regulatory genes is enriched rather than depleted for 4mC (Fig. S16c, d). Non-regulatory genes showed no comparable collapse (0.821 → 0.845). For 6mA, regulatory gene protection was deepest at T2 (ratio = 0.732), with partial recovery at T3 (0.764). These temporal dynamics suggest that the protection zone undergoes developmental-stage-specific reorganization, with 4mC protection collapsing selectively in regulatory genes at stationary phase.

## A sharp 293 bp boundary defines the Shielded/Exposed dichotomy

The protection zone creates a remarkably sharp binary classification among regulatory genes. Of 1,055 regulatory genes analyzed, 993 (94.1%) maintain a methylation-free zone around their TSS ("shielded regulators"), with a median nearest methylation site distance of 762 bp. The remaining 62 (5.9%) lack this protection entirely ("exposed regulators"), with methylation sites directly at their TSS (median distance = 114 bp) and an 8.4-fold higher TSS methylation density (3.15 vs 0.37 sites per kb per gene) (Fig. 2c). It is important to note that the "nearest methylation distance" measured here is the genomic distance from the TSS to the closest experimentally detected DNA methylation modification site — a metric of local epigenomic accessibility — and should not be confused with the distance from the TSS to the translation initiation site (i.e., the 5' UTR length).

The separation between these two classes is statistically overwhelming (Mann-Whitney *U* test, *p* = 5.3 x 10^-28), with a 6.7-fold ratio in median nearest methylation distance. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified an optimal distance threshold of 293 bp (Youden's *J* statistic), achieving 100% sensitivity and 80.6% specificity (AUC = 0.917, 95% CI: 0.895–0.935) (Fig. 3b). This near-perfect single-feature classification demonstrates that the Shielded/Exposed dichotomy is a binary structural property of the regulatory genome, not a graded continuum.

The two classes differ not only in methylation proximity but also in their transcriptional behavior. None of the 62 exposed regulators showed constitutive expression across the three developmental stages (0%), compared to 38% of shielded regulators (odds ratio = infinity, *p* = 5.3 x 10^-13 by Fisher's exact test). The exposed regulators also showed 1.7-fold larger absolute expression changes (median |LFC| T3 vs T1: 1.53 for exposed vs 0.93 for shielded), consistent with their role as dynamic regulators at the developmental boundary. The exposed set showed weak but significant enrichment in the arm regions (56.5% core vs 63.8% core for shielded), though this difference did not reach significance (*p* = 0.277). TF family composition differed between the two classes: exposed regulators were enriched for HTH (other) and sensor kinase annotations, reflecting the diversity of uncharacterized regulators in this set (Table S5).

An important question is whether exposed regulators represent evolutionarily recent additions to the genome — genes that have not yet evolved protection — or established regulatory genes that happen to reside in methylation-accessible genomic contexts. All available evolutionary proxy measures indicate the latter. Exposed and shielded regulators showed no significant difference in SCO locus tag assignment rate (96.8% vs 97.8%), named product rate (100% vs 100%), GC content at third codon positions (0.919 vs 0.932, *p* = 0.060), effective number of codons (31.6 vs 31.5, *p* = 0.725), or rare codon frequency (0.032 vs 0.027, *p* = 0.122). A composite conservation score yielded an ROC AUC of 0.459 — below random chance — confirming that evolutionary divergence metrics have no power to predict exposed status (Fig. S7). The Shielded/Exposed distinction is thus determined by the local epigenomic context — the density and proximity of R-M recognition sites relative to the TSS — not by the gene's evolutionary history or functional importance.

## 62 exposed regulators form a distributed developmental switch

If the 62 exposed regulators are the sole conduit through which methylation interacts with the transcriptional program, understanding their internal organization and biological function becomes essential. Co-expression analysis across all timepoints and replicates revealed that these 62 genes are not a monolithic group but contain strong internal structure organized into two antagonistic blocs (Fig. 4a).

Hierarchical clustering of the 62 x 62 Spearman co-expression matrix identified four modules covering 61 of 62 genes (one sigma factor gene unassigned). Within-type co-expression (gene pairs in the same bloc) was strongly positive (median *rho* = 0.500, *p* = 6.6 x 10^-25), while between-type co-expression (gene pairs across blocs) was negative (median *rho* = -0.283), producing the characteristic mirror-image temporal trajectories of two antagonistic gene sets. Module-internal coherence was even stronger (median *rho* = 0.717, *p* = 1.0 x 10^-19), confirming that the four modules represent tightly coordinated sub-networks. Importantly, these 62 genes are not physically clustered on the chromosome (genomic clustering *z* = +0.30, *p* = 0.37), include zero operonic pairs (*p* = 1.0), and are distributed across both core and arm regions. Their co-regulation arises through trans-acting mechanisms rather than genomic proximity, distinguishing this regulatory layer from the pathway-specific gene clusters common in *Streptomyces*.

The **activation bloc** (~35 genes, modules 1–3) is enriched for regulators of developmental programs. This bloc includes nine TCS components (25% of bloc members), five sigma factors, one WhiB-family regulator, and the aerial mycelium regulator *ramR*. The mean LFC (T3 vs T1) for the activation bloc was +2.47, indicating strong collective upregulation during development. The most dramatically induced genes include *ramR* (SCO6685, LFC = +7.9), encoding a key activator of aerial mycelium formation that controls the production of the surfactant peptide SapB; *nsdB* (LFC = +7.8), a negative regulator of differentiation whose de-repression may serve as a developmental checkpoint; and *SCO1160* (LFC = +5.6), encoding a sensor kinase whose cognate response regulator is shielded (Fig. 4d, Fig. 6b). Additional notable members include multiple sigma-70 family sigma factors that could direct RNA polymerase to developmental promoters, and WhiB-like regulators involved in sporulation. The activation bloc collectively drives the onset of morphogenesis, signal transduction cascades, and stress response programs.

The **repression bloc** (~26 genes, module 4) is enriched for regulators of vegetative growth functions. The mean LFC (T3 vs T1) was -1.68, reflecting coordinated downregulation. The TetR family of transcriptional regulators is the only statistically significantly enriched family in this bloc (odds ratio = 0.16, *p* = 0.028 by Fisher's exact test), with seven TetR members present. This is consistent with the established role of TetR-family proteins in regulating efflux pumps, metabolite transport, and small-molecule resistance — functions characteristic of active vegetative growth that must be shut down during the transition to development. The repression bloc also includes GntR, IclR, and LacI family regulators involved in metabolic control, as well as SSB and HU proteins participating in DNA maintenance and repair (Fig. 4d, Fig. 6a). The coordinated downregulation of this bloc corresponds to the shutdown of primary metabolism, DNA repair capacity, and active efflux at the onset of the developmental program.

Given the prominence of TCS components among the 62 exposed regulators (9 of 62, 14.5%), we examined whether cognate TCS pairs — in which a sensor kinase (SK) is paired with a response regulator (RR) — show coordinated or asymmetric exposed/shielded status. Among the 62 exposed regulators, seven are members of cognate TCS pairs. In every case (7/7, 100%), exactly one member of the pair is exposed while its cognate partner is shielded (Fig. 4e). The probability of this perfect asymmetry occurring by chance is less than 10^-3 (binomial test). Among the exposed partners, the split is 4 SK to 3 RR, showing no bias toward either component type (*p* = 1.0). This asymmetric arrangement is functionally significant: in a TCS, the sensor kinase typically detects an environmental signal while the response regulator executes the transcriptional response. The selective exposure of one component per pair suggests that methylation accessibility may gate the sensory capacity of these signaling systems at the developmental boundary, while leaving the response machinery constitutively available.

To characterize the functional landscape of genes flanked by each methylation type, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the three gene proximity classes: GCCGGC-proximal (n = 3,197), AAGCCCG-proximal (n = 459), and dual-targeted (n = 448). GCCGGC-proximal genes were significantly enriched for the KEGG Quorum Sensing pathway (65/100 pathway genes, enrichment fold = 1.55, FDR = 2.5 × 10^-4) and showed a trend toward Two-Component System enrichment (fold = 1.32, FDR = 0.139), consistent with R-M defense activity concentrated around signaling genes. Dual-targeted genes were enriched for siderophore biosynthesis (5/7 genes, fold = 12.2, FDR = 8.7 × 10^-4), oxidative phosphorylation, and peptidoglycan biosynthesis — functions associated with vegetative growth maintenance. AAGCCCG-proximal genes showed no significant GO or KEGG enrichment, consistent with the uniform geographic distribution of 6mA sites.

## Both blocs activate simultaneously at the vegetative-to-developmental transition

The temporal dynamics of the 62 exposed regulators reveal a coordinated, simultaneous switch at the T1-to-T2 developmental boundary (Fig. 6c). We quantified the timing of each gene's response using a phase ratio metric, defined as the proportion of total expression change occurring during the T1-to-T2 transition relative to the full T1-to-T3 change. The majority of exposed regulators (63%, 39/62 genes) are "early responders" (phase ratio > 0.6), with the bulk of their transcriptional shift concentrated at the exponential-to-transition boundary (Fig. 4c). A smaller fraction showed gradual (8 genes) or late (12 genes) responses, and only 2 genes were non-responders.

The activation bloc transitions from low expression at T1 (mean *z*-score = -1.03) through intermediate levels at T2 (mean *z*-score = +0.23) to high expression at T3 (mean *z*-score = +0.79), with a mean LFC of +1.91 at the T1-to-T2 transition and +0.53 at T2-to-T3 (Fig. 4b). The repression bloc shows the mirror-image trajectory: high at T1 (mean *z*-score = +1.28), declining through T2 (mean *z*-score = -0.48) to low at T3 (mean *z*-score = -0.81), with a mean LFC of -1.47 at T1-to-T2 and -0.21 at T2-to-T3. Critically, both blocs engage at the same time — a Mann-Whitney *U* test comparing phase ratios between blocs yielded *p* = 0.459, indicating no temporal separation between activation and repression (Fig. 4c). This simultaneity is consistent with a single "gate-opening" event at the developmental boundary rather than a sequential cascade in which one bloc triggers the other. Module-internal temporal coherence was high (Spearman *rho* = 0.717, *p* = 1.0 x 10^-19), confirming that genes within each bloc respond as coordinated units. However, cross-module temporal prediction was essentially zero, reinforcing the conclusion that the modules respond in parallel to a shared developmental signal.

Notably, the timing of methylation changes was decoupled from the timing of expression changes. The correlation between the magnitude of methylation change and expression change was not significant at either developmental transition (T1-T2: *rho* = 0.136, *p* = 0.299; T2-T3: *rho* = 0.012, *p* = 0.927). This temporal independence indicates that methylation provides the structural context — defining which genes are exposed to the regulatory environment — but does not provide the temporal signal that triggers the switch. The developmental "gate" opens once at the vegetative-to-developmental boundary, and the exposed regulators respond to developmental signals (likely metabolic, nutritional, or cell density-dependent cues) that are themselves independent of methylation dynamics.

## Negative results define the model's boundaries

The Gatekeeper model was shaped as much by rejected hypotheses as by supported ones. We present four categories of negative results that are essential for defining the model's scope and ruling out alternative explanations (Fig. 5).

**Simpson's paradox eliminates apparent genome-wide methylation-expression correlations.** We identified three independent instances of Simpson's paradox, all driven by the linear chromosome's core/arm functional differentiation (Fig. 5a, Fig. S2). The apparent suppressive effect of GCCGGC proximity on gene expression (*p* = 4.1 x 10^-10, unstratified) vanished when analysis was restricted to core-region genes (*p* = 0.87). The apparent de-repression of genes losing GCCGGC methylation (*p* = 8.3 x 10^-8, unstratified) was similarly eliminated by geographic stratification (core-only *p* = 0.87). A third instance involved the apparent enrichment of methylation sites in biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) (fold = 1.66, *p* = 4.4 x 10^-8, unstratified), which reduced to fold = 1.07 (not significant) in the core-only analysis — a consequence of the near-complete co-localization of both methylation sites and BGCs in the chromosomal core at T1. In each case, the confound has the same structure: a non-random spatial distribution of methylation sites along the chromosome combined with a non-random spatial distribution of gene functional properties produces a spurious correlation. The AAGCCCG system, which has minimal geographic bias (4.7 percentage-point shift), served as an internal control: its de-repression test yielded *p* = 0.91 with no geographic confound (Fig. 5b). These results establish that any methylation-expression analysis in organisms with linear chromosomes and core/arm functional differentiation must include geographic stratification (e.g., Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test) before causal claims can be made.

**DNA sequence enrichment does not equal methylation enrichment.** The AAGCCCG recognition motif is 5.0-fold enriched in the DNA sequence within ±300 bp of exposed TF TSSs compared to shielded TF TSSs, with 25.8% (~16/62) of exposed TFs carrying the motif near their TSS (Fig. 5c). This initially suggested that AAGCCCG methylation might directly mediate exposed TF regulation. However, only 3.2% (2/62) of exposed TFs have a methylated AAGCCCG site within ±500 bp of their TSS, and these two genes show expression changes in opposite directions. The eight-fold discrepancy between sequence presence (25.8%) and methylation presence (3.2%) arises because the AAGCCCG methyltransferase methylates only approximately 19.5% of its recognition sites genome-wide — a selectivity that is unexplained but consistent with the behavior of many Type I R-M systems. The sequence enrichment thus reflects an evolutionary or structural property of exposed regulatory DNA — reduced counter-selection against the motif, as expected for genes that lack the protection zone — rather than active methylation-mediated regulation. This distinction between sequence enrichment and functional methylation is critical and cautions against inferring regulatory mechanisms from motif frequency alone.

**Exposed regulators act in trans, not in cis.** In *Streptomyces*, pathway-specific regulators typically reside within or adjacent to the gene clusters they regulate (e.g., ActII-ORF4 within the actinorhodin BGC). If exposed TFs function similarly as cis-regulators of adjacent genes, their genomic neighbors should show correlated expression changes. A permutation test (10,000 permutations) showed no evidence for this: the observed mean absolute LFC of neighbors (±10 genes from each exposed TF) was well within the null distribution (*p* = 0.857) (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, there was no distance decay in the correlation between an exposed TF's expression change and that of its neighbors (Spearman *rho* = +0.026, not significant). The transcriptional concordance rate of exposed TF neighbors (59.9%) was not significantly higher than that of shielded TF neighbors (55.6%, *p* = 0.191). These results establish that the 62 exposed regulators exert their effects through trans-acting mechanisms on genomically dispersed targets, distinguishing them from the cis-regulatory paradigm that dominates secondary metabolism regulation in *Streptomyces*.

**The proximate regulatory mechanism remains unidentified.** Despite the strong evidence for coordinated regulation and functional coherence, the mechanism through which the 62 exposed TFs exert their regulatory effects could not be identified computationally. None of the 62 exposed TFs have curated binding motifs in available databases (0/62 in FIMO analysis using CollecTF and CIS-BP databases). While 38 of 62 are themselves targets of known TFs identified by FIMO, this rate is indistinguishable from that of shielded regulators (odds ratio = 0.881, *p* = 0.683), ruling out preferential targeting by master regulators as the coordination mechanism. The AAGCCCG methylation mechanism was eliminated as described above (2/62 methylated). The neighborhood effect was eliminated by permutation analysis. The upstream TF regulation model was also tested: target versus non-target absolute LFC showed no difference (*p* = 0.891), indicating that known TF cascades do not drive the observed coordination. We conclude that the 62 exposed regulators constitute a parallel regulatory layer that operates independently of all computationally characterized transcriptional networks in *S. coelicolor*. Identification of the proximate mechanism — whether through direct methylation-sensitive DNA binding, protein-protein interactions with methylation-sensitive complexes, or an as-yet-uncharacterized developmental signal — will require experimental approaches including chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq), and targeted genetic knockouts of key exposed regulators such as *ramR* and *nsdB*.

# Discussion

## Summary of the Gatekeeper model

This study reveals that DNA methylation does not function as a direct transcriptional regulator in *S. coelicolor* M145. Instead, we describe a four-layer "Gatekeeper" architecture that governs how two R-M methylation systems interact with the regulatory genome. The R-M systems undergo dramatic geographic redistribution during development — with GCCGGC 4mC sites showing complete positional turnover (Jaccard = 0.000) from core to arm regions — but this remodeling has no detectable effect on gene expression once geographic confounds are accounted for (GCCGGC core-only *p* = 0.87; AAGCCCG *r* = 0.003, *p* = 0.91). The vast majority of regulatory gene promoters (993/1,055, 94.1%) maintain a ~2,200 bp methylation-free protection zone through the combined action of evolutionary counter-selection of recognition motifs (~33%) and collective protein occupancy at promoters (~67%). The remaining 62 regulatory genes (5.9%) lack this protection entirely, with methylation sites directly at their TSSs. These exposed regulators are not evolutionary newcomers but well-conserved regulatory genes (composite conservation AUC = 0.459, below random) whose exposed status is determined by local epigenomic context. They are organized into two antagonistic blocs — an activation bloc (~35 genes) driving developmental programs and a repression bloc (~26 genes) shutting down vegetative programs — that engage simultaneously at the exponential-to-transition boundary (phase separation *p* = 0.459), constituting a distributed developmental switch.

## Reframing bacterial methylation-transcription interactions

The prevailing view of bacterial DNA methylation assigns it dual roles: phage defense through R-M systems and gene regulation through orphan methyltransferases or R-M-associated methylation at specific loci [CITE: Casadesus and Low, 2006; Sanchez-Romero and Casadesus, 2020]. The Dam methylase of *E. coli* and CcrM of *C. crescentus* provide compelling evidence for direct regulatory roles at individual loci, and these examples have fostered an expectation that methylation-expression correlations observed in genome-wide studies reflect regulatory relationships.

Our findings challenge this extrapolation. In *S. coelicolor*, the entire apparent genome-wide methylation-expression correlation is a statistical artifact — a Simpson's paradox generated by the non-random co-localization of methylation sites and functionally distinct gene classes along the linear chromosome. The true relationship between methylation and transcription operates not through direct regulation of gene expression but through a structural partitioning of the regulatory genome into a methylation-insensitive majority and a methylation-responsive minority. This distinction is critical: the 993 shielded regulators show no methylation-dependent expression changes (Spearman *rho* = 0.063, *p* = 0.051), while the 62 exposed regulators show a modest but significant correlation (*rho* = 0.277, *p* = 0.029). Methylation's regulatory influence is thus channeled through a narrow bottleneck of 5.9% of regulatory genes, rather than operating genome-wide as previously assumed for many bacterial methylation systems.

This structural partitioning model may extend beyond *Streptomyces*. Any bacterium with non-random chromosomal organization — including species with replication-coupled methylation biases, origin-proximal/distal gene expression gradients, or macrodomain-structured chromosomes — may harbor analogous confounds that produce spurious methylation-expression correlations. We propose that geographic stratification (e.g., using the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test) should become a standard requirement for methylation-expression association studies in bacteria, particularly in organisms with established chromosomal functional differentiation. Furthermore, the distinction between R-M defense methylation and regulatory methylation should be more carefully considered: the mere presence of temporal methylation dynamics does not imply regulatory function, as we demonstrate for both GCCGGC and AAGCCCG systems.

## Three-dimensional chromosome reorganization provides a physical framework

The geographic redistribution of GCCGGC sites from core (83% at T1) to arms (82% at T2) raises the question of what physical mechanism drives such a dramatic positional shift during a single developmental transition. Recent Hi-C analysis of *S. coelicolor* M145 has revealed that the linear chromosome undergoes large-scale three-dimensional reorganization during the growth-phase shift, with changes in chromosomal interaction domains (CIDs) and global compaction patterns that correlate with transcriptional reprogramming [CITE: Deng et al., 2023]. Specifically, the core region — which is highly interactive and transcriptionally active during vegetative growth — undergoes compaction changes at the transition to secondary metabolism, while the arm regions show altered accessibility patterns.

This chromosome-level structural remodeling provides a parsimonious explanation for the GCCGGC geographic shift. During vegetative growth (T1), the core region may be physically accessible to the GCCGGC methyltransferase due to an open chromatin-like configuration, resulting in the observed 83% core localization of 4mC sites. At the developmental transition (T2), CID reorganization and differential compaction could restrict methyltransferase access to the core while opening the arm regions, producing the inverted geographic distribution (82% arm). The complete non-overlap between T1 and T2 site sets (Jaccard = 0.000) is consistent with a model in which methylation sites are re-established de novo at each developmental stage according to the prevailing chromosomal accessibility landscape, rather than being inherited through replication. This interpretation links the epigenomic dynamics observed in our study to the higher-order chromosome architecture described by Hi-C, suggesting that the R-M methylation landscape is shaped by — and reports on — the three-dimensional organization of the chromosome.

The spatiotemporal dynamics of nucleoid-associated proteins further support this framework. HupA, one of the two HU-family NAPs in *S. coelicolor*, is expressed predominantly in vegetative hyphae and localizes to the chromosomal core [CITE: Salerno et al., 2009], coinciding with the GCCGGC core-enrichment at T1. HupS, by contrast, is developmentally regulated and expressed specifically during sporulation, with a role in chromosome arm organization and spore DNA packaging [CITE: Salerno et al., 2009]. The transition from HupA-dominated (core, vegetative) to HupS-dominated (arm, sporulation) nucleoid architecture parallels the GCCGGC core-to-arm shift, suggesting that the redistribution of methylation sites reflects the handover between distinct NAP regimes that differentially compact and protect chromosomal regions during development.

## The protection zone: parallels with eukaryotic chromatin organization

The 2,200 bp methylation-free zone around regulatory gene promoters in *S. coelicolor* is reminiscent of CpG islands in vertebrate genomes, where promoter-associated regions are depleted for CpG methylation and enriched for active histone marks [CITE: Deaton and Bird, 2011]. In both cases, the protection is associated with regulatory function and appears to involve a combination of sequence-level features and protein occupancy. The parallel extends further: just as CpG island methylation in vertebrates is associated with gene silencing, we find that the absence of the protection zone (exposed status) is associated with dynamic, development-dependent expression rather than constitutive expression.

However, the mechanism of protection differs fundamentally from eukaryotic chromatin-based regulation. In *S. coelicolor*, individual TF binding sites do not create local methylation exclusion — indeed, GCCGGC methylation is slightly enriched at predicted TF BSs (fold = 1.157). Instead, the protection appears to arise from the constitutive protein architecture of the promoter region: a collective assembly of nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs), basal transcription factors, and RNA polymerase that sterically occludes methyltransferase access regardless of whether the gene is actively transcribed. This interpretation is supported by the expression-independence of protection (baseMean AUC = 0.547): even infrequently transcribed regulatory genes maintain their methylation-free zone, indicating that the resident protein complement — not the act of transcription itself — provides the barrier.

The NAP complement of *S. coelicolor* includes proteins with diverse DNA-binding properties. HupA and IHF bind DNA with limited sequence preference, providing broad coverage of promoter regions [CITE: Smits and Grossman, 2010; Guo et al., 2021], while Lsr2 preferentially targets AT-rich DNA in actinobacteria [CITE: Guo et al., 2021]. Gbn, a recently characterized NAP specific to Actinobacteria, recognizes the GATCWT motif with >2,800 chromosomal binding regions encompassing ~55% of all GATCWT sites in the genome [CITE: Du et al., 2022]. The protection zone thus cannot be described as entirely sequence-independent; rather, it reflects collective, redundant shielding by multiple NAPs whose binding territories overlap at regulatory gene promoters. The spatiotemporal dynamics of these NAPs are particularly revealing: HupA predominates during vegetative growth with core-region localization, whereas HupS is upregulated during sporulation and participates in arm-region chromosome packaging [CITE: Salerno et al., 2009]. This HupA-to-HupS handover mirrors the GCCGGC core-to-arm shift (T1 to T2), raising the possibility that developmental changes in NAP composition alter not only chromosome compaction but also the local methylation protection landscape. This predicts that ChIP-seq experiments targeting individual NAPs would show differential enrichment at shielded versus exposed TSSs, with the specific NAP profile varying across developmental stages — providing a direct experimental test of this aspect of the model.

The two-tier nature of the protection — ~33% evolutionary counter-selection, ~67% protein occupancy — suggests that these mechanisms are partially redundant and have co-evolved. Regulatory genes have evolved reduced R-M motif density in their coding sequences and promoter regions (fold = 0.74–0.76), providing a basal level of protection that is reinforced by the physical barrier of promoter-bound proteins. This dual defense may explain why the Shielded/Exposed dichotomy is so sharp (AUC = 0.917): both mechanisms must fail simultaneously for a regulatory gene to be exposed, and the combined probability of this double failure is low (5.9%).

## The 62 exposed regulators: a parallel regulatory layer

Perhaps the most unexpected finding is that the 62 exposed regulators do not correspond to any previously characterized regulatory pathway in *S. coelicolor*. None of the 62 have curated binding motifs (0/62 FIMO), and they are not preferentially targeted by known master regulators (OR = 0.881, *p* = 0.683). The well-characterized developmental cascades — BldA/BldH, WhiB/WhiG, the sigma factor networks — operate through the shielded regulatory genome. The exposed regulators constitute a parallel layer that has escaped previous characterization precisely because it operates outside known regulatory networks and was identified only through integrated epigenome-transcriptome analysis.

The internal organization of this parallel layer is striking. The two-bloc structure — activation (TCS, sigma factors, RamR) versus repression (TetR, metabolism, DNA repair) — maps precisely onto the functional requirements of the vegetative-to-developmental transition. The activation bloc includes *ramR* (LFC = +7.9), one of the strongest known activators of aerial mycelium formation, and five sigma factors that could direct RNA polymerase to developmental promoters. The repression bloc is enriched for TetR-family regulators (OR = 0.16, *p* = 0.028), a family with well-established roles in regulating efflux, transport, and small-molecule metabolism — the hallmarks of active vegetative growth. The simultaneous engagement of both blocs (*p* = 0.459 for temporal separation) suggests that the developmental transition is not a sequential cascade but a coordinated gate-opening event in which vegetative functions are shut down and developmental functions are activated in parallel.

The perfect asymmetry of TCS pairs (7/7 split between exposed and shielded) provides additional insight into the organizational logic of this system. In each cognate pair, exactly one component is exposed to methylation while its partner is shielded. This arrangement could enable the methylation system to selectively gate the developmental-sensing function of TCS systems while leaving the constitutive signaling component protected. The 4:3 split between exposed sensor kinases and response regulators shows no component-type bias, suggesting that the gating operates at the level of the pair rather than at a specific component type. This asymmetric TCS architecture has not been reported previously and may represent a general mechanism for coupling epigenomic context to signal transduction specificity.

## Simpson's paradox: a cautionary tale for epigenomics

The identification of three independent Simpson's paradox instances in a single organism carries methodological implications beyond *S. coelicolor*. The confound in each case has the same structure: a non-random distribution of methylation sites along the chromosome combined with a non-random distribution of gene functional properties produces a spurious, statistically significant correlation (*p* = 4.1 x 10^-10 in the most extreme case) that is biologically plausible and would be accepted as genuine without geographic stratification.

Linear chromosomes with core/arm differentiation are widespread in Actinobacteria, including many medically and industrially important *Streptomyces*, *Nocardia*, and *Rhodococcus* species [CITE: Chen et al., 2002]. Circular chromosomes with origin-proximal/distal differentiation (e.g., *E. coli* macrodomains) may harbor analogous, though potentially less severe, confounds [CITE: Valens et al., 2004]. The AAGCCCG result in our study — a clean null with minimal geographic bias — demonstrates that not all methylation systems are equally susceptible to this confound. The severity depends on the degree of geographic bias in methylation site distribution, which should be assessed before any causal interpretation of methylation-expression correlations. We recommend that all bacterial epigenomic studies report the geographic distribution of methylation sites and perform stratified analyses before claiming direct regulatory roles.

## Limitations and future directions

Several limitations constrain the current analysis. First, all conclusions are based on correlational evidence from three developmental timepoints; causal relationships cannot be established without genetic perturbation experiments. The most critical experiment is the construction and characterization of methyltransferase knockout strains: if the Gatekeeper model is correct, removing methylation should alter the expression of exposed but not shielded regulators, and the distinction between the two classes should collapse. Second, the proximate mechanism by which exposed TFs exert their coordinated regulatory effects remains unknown. DAP-seq or ChIP-seq for key exposed regulators (particularly RamR, NsdB, and top TCS components) would reveal their downstream targets and potentially the basis for their coordination. Third, the analysis is limited to strain M145; comparative nanopore methylome analysis across *Streptomyces* species would test whether the Shielded/Exposed dichotomy is a conserved organizational principle, as suggested by the equal conservation of exposed and shielded regulators (composite conservation *p* = 0.276). Fourth, the three-timepoint design may miss transient methylation states or rapid expression dynamics at the developmental boundary; higher temporal resolution sampling could determine whether the "simultaneous switch" (phase separation *p* = 0.459) reflects true simultaneity or a rapid cascade below our temporal detection limit.

Despite these limitations, the Gatekeeper model provides a coherent, quantitative framework that accounts for the full complexity of methylation-transcription interactions in *S. coelicolor* M145 and generates specific, testable predictions for experimental validation. The central insight — that bacterial R-M methylation can organize the regulatory genome through structural partitioning rather than direct transcriptional control — may represent a general principle applicable to other bacteria with active R-M systems, offering a new perspective on the functional interplay between defense systems and gene regulation in prokaryotes.

# Methods

## Bacterial strain and growth conditions

*Streptomyces coelicolor* A3(2) strain M145 was cultured under standard conditions. Three developmental stages were sampled: exponential growth (T1), transition phase (T2), and stationary phase (T3), each with three biological replicates (nine samples total).

## Nanopore sequencing and DNA methylation detection

Genomic DNA was sequenced using Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) long-read sequencing. Reads were aligned to the *S. coelicolor* A3(2) M145 reference genome (NCBI RefSeq GCF_000203835.1) using minimap2 with the `-y` flag to preserve base modification tags (MM, ML, MN). Sequencing coverage ranged from 14 to 52x per sample (eight samples at 27–52x; one sample at 14.3x).

DNA methylation was called using modkit (ONT) in pileup mode, generating per-site methylation calls from aligned BAM files. Two modification types were detected with high confidence: N4-methylcytosine (4mC; 3,294 total sites) and N6-methyladenine (6mA; 2,734 total sites). 5-methylcytosine (5mC) was not detected at high confidence. High-confidence methylation sites were defined as sites passing modkit's default quality thresholds. Motif assignment was performed by extracting the sequence context of each methylation site and identifying enriched motifs: GCCGGC for 4mC and AAGCCCG for 6mA.

For samples with initially low coverage (samples 3-2 and 3-4), additional ONT sequencing data were generated and merged with existing alignments using a standardized pipeline to improve methylation calling confidence.

## Methylation site classification by timepoint

Methylation sites were classified by the timepoints at which they were detected. "T1 sites" were those called as methylated at T1 (with or without methylation at later timepoints); "T2 sites" and "T3 sites" were defined analogously. "Lost" sites were those methylated at T1 but not at T2; "Never" sites were those not methylated at either timepoint. Transition categories (e.g., concordant_loss, discordant_gain_up) were assigned based on the combination of methylation status change and expression change direction.

## RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from each of the nine samples (three timepoints x three biological replicates). Paired-end Illumina sequencing (150 bp reads) was performed. Raw reads were quality-controlled and adapter-trimmed using fastp.

## RNA-seq alignment and quantification

Trimmed reads were aligned to the *S. coelicolor* A3(2) M145 reference genome (NCBI RefSeq GCF_000203835.1) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 in prokaryotic mode (no spliced alignment). Overall alignment rate was 98.54%, with 96.55% uniquely mapped reads. Gene-level read counts were quantified using featureCounts.

## Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with the design formula `~ condition`, where condition corresponds to the three developmental stages (T1, T2, T3). Size factor normalization was applied using the default DESeq2 method. Log2 fold change (LFC) estimates were shrunk using the apeglm method. Three pairwise contrasts were computed: T2 vs T1, T3 vs T1, and T3 vs T2. Genes with fewer than 10 counts in fewer than 3 samples were filtered prior to analysis, yielding 7,646 genes tested. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted *p* < 0.05 and |LFC| > 1. Mean normalized expression (baseMean) was computed as the average of DESeq2-normalized counts across all nine samples.

## Regulatory gene annotation

Regulatory genes were identified through a two-stage process. First, 873 genes were automatically classified as regulators based on product annotation pattern matching against known regulatory protein families (TFs, TCS components, sigma factors). Second, nine additional genes were manually curated based on established literature: *actII-orf4* (SCO5082), *redD* (SCO5877), *redZ* (SCO5881), *absA1* (SCO3225), *absA2* (SCO3226), *kasO* (SCO6282), *scbR* (SCO6265), *scbR2* (SCO6286), and *atrA* (SCO4677). Regulatory genes were further classified into families: sigma factors (76 genes), TCS sensor kinases (83), TCS response regulators (81), SARP-family activators (7), and other TFs (786). The final set comprised 1,055 regulatory genes with assigned TSS positions.

## Transcription start site assignment

TSS positions were assigned by integrating two data sources. The primary source was the gene start coordinate from the NCBI RefSeq GFF annotation. Where available, experimentally determined TSS positions from dRNA-seq data (Jeong et al. 2016) were used instead (262 genes with experimental TSS data). For genes on the minus strand, distance calculations were adjusted so that negative values indicate upstream positions and positive values indicate positions within the gene body. The final comprehensive TSS table covered 7,996 protein-coding genes.

## TSS-centered methylation density profiles

Methylation density profiles were computed in 200 bp bins spanning ±5 kb from each TSS. For each bin, the number of methylation sites per kb per gene was calculated separately for regulatory genes, non-regulatory genes, and all genes. Gaussian smoothing (sigma = 2 bins, effective 400 bp) was applied to reduce noise. Bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples) were computed for each bin. The protection zone was defined as the contiguous region where regulatory gene methylation density was depleted relative to flanking regions, with the depletion ratio calculated as the density within the zone divided by the mean density of flanking regions (±3–5 kb).

## Shielded/Exposed classification

For each of the 1,055 regulatory genes, the nearest methylation site distance was computed as the minimum distance between any high-confidence methylation site (across all timepoints and types) and the gene's TSS. ROC analysis was performed using the nearest methylation distance as a predictor of a binary classification variable. The optimal threshold was determined using Youden's *J* statistic (maximizing sensitivity + specificity - 1). AUC with 95% CI was computed using bootstrap resampling. Genes with nearest methylation distance below the optimal threshold (293 bp) were classified as "exposed"; all others were classified as "shielded." The classification was validated by comparison with baseMean (mean expression level) as a negative control predictor. Expression quintile analysis used the Jonckheere-Terpstra (JT) test for trend.

## Sequence determinants of protection

R-M recognition motif density was quantified in the DNA sequence (independent of methylation status) within ±300 bp of each regulatory gene TSS. The AAGCCCG and TGGCCGGC (GCCGGC palindrome) motifs were scanned using exact string matching. Enrichment at exposed versus shielded TF promoters was tested using Fisher's exact test. A logistic regression model incorporating all sequence features (individual motif counts and combined features) was trained to predict exposed/shielded status, with performance evaluated by 5-fold cross-validation AUC. The sequence contribution to the Shielded/Exposed dichotomy was estimated as the ratio of the sequence model's cross-validated AUC to the nearest methylation distance AUC.

## TF binding site methylation analysis

Known TF binding motifs were obtained from CollecTF and CIS-BP databases and scanned across the M145 genome using FIMO (Find Individual Motif Occurrences). Methylation density profiles were computed centered on predicted TF BSs (±2 kb), analogous to the TSS-centered profiles. The enrichment fold at TF BSs was calculated as the observed methylation density divided by the expected density from randomly positioned control sites, matched for GC content and chromosomal position.

## Geographic stratification and Simpson's paradox analysis

The *S. coelicolor* linear chromosome was divided into core (1.5–7.17 Mb) and arm regions (0–1.5 Mb, left arm; 7.17–8.67 Mb, right arm) based on established genomic architecture. For each methylation-expression association test, analyses were performed three ways: unstratified (all genes), core-only, and arm-only. The Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) test was used for geographic stratification of categorical methylation-expression associations. For continuous outcomes (LFC comparisons between Lost and Never gene sets), Mann-Whitney *U* tests were performed separately within each geographic stratum. Simpson's paradox was diagnosed when a significant unstratified association became non-significant in both geographic strata.

## Co-expression analysis and module detection

A 62 x 62 Spearman correlation matrix was computed for the 62 exposed regulators using rlog-transformed expression values across all nine samples. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Ward's linkage method with Euclidean distance on the correlation matrix. Modules were identified by cutting the dendrogram at a level that maximized within-module coherence while maintaining biologically interpretable group sizes. Blocs were assigned by merging modules with concordant temporal trajectories: modules with net positive LFC (T3 vs T1) were assigned to the activation bloc, and modules with net negative LFC were assigned to the repression bloc.

Within-type and between-type co-expression medians were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Genomic clustering was assessed by computing the mean pairwise chromosomal distance between exposed regulators and comparing it to a null distribution from 10,000 random gene sets of equal size. Operonic pair status was determined from operon predictions in the reference annotation.

## Temporal dynamics analysis

For each of the 62 exposed regulators, expression values were *z*-score normalized across the three timepoints. The phase ratio was defined as the proportion of total absolute expression change (|LFC_T3vsT1|) attributable to the T1-to-T2 transition: phase ratio = |LFC_T2vsT1| / (|LFC_T2vsT1| + |LFC_T3vsT2|). Genes with phase ratio > 0.6 were classified as "early responders." The Mann-Whitney *U* test was used to compare phase ratio distributions between the activation and repression blocs. Module-internal temporal coherence was assessed by computing the median Spearman correlation of temporal trajectories (T1, T2, T3 *z*-scores) among all gene pairs within each module. Methylation-expression timing correlation was assessed by Spearman correlation between the magnitude of methylation change and expression change at each transition (T1-T2 and T2-T3).

## TF family enrichment analysis

TF family annotations were assigned based on protein domain architecture and product description. For each TF family represented among the 62 exposed regulators, Fisher's exact test was used to assess enrichment in the activation versus repression bloc. The TetR family was the only family achieving statistical significance after considering multiple comparisons.

For TCS pair analysis, cognate SK-RR pairs were identified from genomic adjacency and domain complementarity. The asymmetry of exposed/shielded status within pairs was tested using a binomial test (null: 50% probability of both members sharing the same status). The SK/RR composition of exposed partners was tested for bias using a binomial test (null: 50% SK).

## Neighborhood expression analysis

For each exposed TF, the mean absolute LFC (T3 vs T1) of flanking genes (±10 genes) was computed. A null distribution was generated by computing the same statistic for 10,000 randomly sampled gene sets of size 62 from the full genome. The permutation *p*-value was the fraction of random sets with mean neighbor |LFC| greater than or equal to the observed value. Distance decay was assessed by Spearman correlation between chromosomal distance (in bp) from the exposed TF and the neighbor's |LFC|. Transcriptional concordance rate was defined as the fraction of neighbors with the same sign of LFC as the adjacent exposed TF.

## Conservation analysis

Evolutionary conservation of exposed versus shielded regulators was assessed using multiple proxy measures available from the genome annotation: (1) SCO locus tag assignment rate (presence in the original *S. coelicolor* annotation), (2) GC content at third codon positions (GC3), (3) effective number of codons (Nc), and (4) rare codon frequency. Each metric was compared between exposed and shielded groups using Mann-Whitney *U* tests. A composite conservation score was computed as the mean of z-scored individual metrics, and ROC analysis was performed to assess the discriminative power of conservation for predicting exposed status.

## Statistical analysis and software

All analyses were performed in Python 3 using NumPy, pandas, SciPy (v1.11+), statsmodels, and scikit-learn. Differential expression analysis used DESeq2 (v1.38+) in R (v4.3+). Visualization was performed using matplotlib and seaborn. Multiple testing correction used the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure where applicable. *p*-values are reported in scientific notation for *p* < 0.001 and in decimal notation for *p* >= 0.001. Effect sizes (AUC, *rho*, odds ratio, fold change) are reported alongside *p*-values throughout. All code is available at [repository URL].
